Connect your adapter

Plug the adapter into the OBD port under the dashboard on the driver's side and switch the ignition on. Then choose how the adapter connects to this computer.

Bluetooth help: adapter not in the list?
  1. Check the adapter. If the box or listing says "Bluetooth 4.0", "BLE" or "works with iPhone", it's Bluetooth LE. Adapters sold "for Android" (e.g. Panlong) are Bluetooth Classic.
  2. Bluetooth Classic: first pair it in Windows (Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Add device → Bluetooth). It usually appears as "OBDII", "OBD2" or "V-LINK". The PIN is normally 1234 or 0000. Then press Bluetooth Classic and pick it from the list. If the list is empty, press USB cable: the paired adapter also appears there as a COM port.
  3. Still not listed? Give it a COM port. Press Windows key, type Bluetooth, open Bluetooth and other devices settings, scroll down and click More Bluetooth settings (Windows 11: Devices → More Bluetooth settings). Open the COM Ports tab → Add… → Outgoing → choose OBDII → OK. Then press USB cable here and pick Standard Serial over Bluetooth link (COMx). If there are two, pick the outgoing one shown on the COM Ports tab.
  4. Adapter powered? It only works while plugged into the car with the ignition on. Its light should be on.
  5. Chrome up to date? Bluetooth serial needs Chrome/Edge 117 or later (Menu → Help → About).
  6. Re-pair: in Windows Bluetooth settings choose the adapter → Remove device, unplug it for 10 seconds, plug it back in and pair again (PIN 1234 or 0000).
  7. Bluetooth LE: don't pair it in Windows. Just press Bluetooth LE and pick it from the list.
  8. If it connects but the car doesn't answer, check the ignition is on, then try another baud rate (38400 first, then 9600 or 115200).
  9. Only one app can use the adapter at a time, so close other OBD apps on your phone or PC.
  10. Wi-Fi adapters (not Bluetooth): join the adapter's Wi-Fi network, run obd-wifi-bridge.py, then press Wi-Fi adapter.

Reading…

Full-system module scan

Finds every control module that answers on the diagnostic CAN bus (engine, gearbox/TCM, ABS/ESP, airbag/SRS, body/BCM, climate and others), then reads each one's name and fault codes using UDS (ISO 14229). Engine off, ignition on. It takes about 30–60 seconds.

Mode 08 – on-board system tests

The only actuator tests standardised for every car. Most cars support just the EVAP leak test (it seals the fuel vapour system), and many support none.

Module actuator control (UDS)

Commands a module directly: 0x2F input/output control (e.g. switch the radiator fan on, pulse an injector) or 0x31 routine control (e.g. ABS pump bleed). The identifiers are different on every make and model, so they must come from the vehicle's service information. Wrong values can move real hardware.

Extended / manufacturer PIDs (Mode 22)

Read OEM-only values such as gearbox temperature, DPF soot load or oil level that aren't in standard OBD2. Add them from a published PID list for your car. Formula letters: A = first data byte, B = second, and so on.

NameModule / DIDValue

Manufacturer broadcast frames

Listens to all traffic on the diagnostic CAN bus for a few seconds. It shows each message ID, how often it's sent, and which bytes change (highlighted), which is how OEM signals are reverse-engineered. Many newer cars have a gateway that keeps the OBD port quiet, so an empty list is normal on those.

Predictive health tracking

Run a 20-second health check each time you use the car. The app keeps a history for this vehicle (by VIN) and watches for slow drift in charging voltage, fuel trims, O2 sensor switching and warm-up temperature, which can warn of a failing part weeks before a warning light. Run it at idle with the engine warm.

Reset the service light

After an oil change or service, reset the reminder on the dashboard. Generic OBD2 can't reset it, so here is the typical procedure using the car's own buttons and menus. Steps vary by model and year: check your owner's handbook if yours differs.

Some cars, and items such as brake pad or DPF counters, can only be reset electronically with a dealer or workshop tool that knows the car's own commands. Advanced users can send manufacturer commands from Active tests.

Tyre check

Generic OBD2 doesn't report tyre pressures (only the car's own tyre-monitoring module knows them), so this is a guided check with a tyre gauge and tread gauge. Check when the tyres are cold, before driving or after less than 2 miles. The scanner judges each tyre, keeps a history and spots slow punctures.

Particulate filter

Diesel cars have a DPF and many newer petrol cars a GPF. Both trap soot, which the engine burns off by heating the filter: a "regeneration". Short trips stop that from finishing, the filter blocks and a warning light comes on. This screen shows the filter's live readings, checks for faults that stop regeneration, and guides you through a regeneration drive.

Filter fault codes

Before you start

Guided regeneration drive

The safest way to clear a partly blocked filter: give the engine the steady, warm running it needs to regenerate by itself. Drive for about 20 minutes at a steady 45 to 60 mph (70 to 100 km/h), keeping the engine above about 2,000 rpm. Don't switch off while it is regenerating. The scanner tracks the drive and tells you when it has finished.

If the filter warning light is flashing, or the car is in reduced power ("limp") mode, don't try a regeneration drive: the filter may be too full to regenerate safely. Have it checked by a garage.

Workshop forced regeneration (advanced) Stationary regeneration commanded through the engine ECU
For experienced users only. A forced regeneration heats the exhaust above 600 °C with the car parked. It needs the regeneration routine ID for your exact engine (from manufacturer service information), and many ECUs also need a manufacturer security login, in which case they will refuse it here. The scanner stops the routine automatically if the car moves, the engine stops, the coolant or the filter gets too hot, or it runs longer than 25 minutes.

Safety checklist

Commands used: 10 03 (extended session), 31 01 <ID> (start routine), 3E 00 (keep session open), 31 03 <ID> (routine status), 31 02 <ID> (stop routine), 10 01 (back to default session). A reply of 7F 31 33 means security access is required.

How the analysis works

The analyser learns from your own car as you drive. For each driving state (idling, pulling away, cruising and slowing, cold or warm) it learns the normal value and spread of each sensor. It also keeps three prediction models that update continuously: airflow from engine speed and manifold pressure, charging voltage from engine speed and load, and fuel trim from load and engine speed. When a reading is far from what the model predicts, that is flagged as unusual for this car, often before it is far enough out to set a fault code.

Conclusions come from diagnostic rules that weigh this evidence over time, so a pattern has to hold for several seconds before it is reported. Confidence then fades again if the pattern stops. Because the models learn your car's normal, a fault that was already there when learning started looks normal to them. The rules check absolute limits too, so those faults are still caught.

Everything stays on this computer and is saved for this car's VIN. Use Reset learning after a repair that changes how the car behaves.

Test centre

Each test sends one read-only command, explains what it asks the car, and compares the answer with what a healthy car returns. Nothing is changed or cleared. For the most complete results, run the tests with the engine warm and idling, then again with the engine off.
